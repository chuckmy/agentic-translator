October on the Common

The October light in Brattleboro had a quality that Eleanor never quite found anywhere else. It came down through the maples on the Common in long, slanted bands, settling on the white clapboard of the old Congregational church and turning even the rusted park benches into something photographable.

She had walked this path every morning for thirty-one years. The bookstore on Main Street, where she had worked since her divorce, opened at ten; before that, the morning was hers — coffee from the diner at the corner, then the loop around the Common, then home to feed the cat.

This morning, though, the bookstore would not open at ten. It would not open at all. Howard, her employer of three decades, had called the night before to say that the lease was up and the building had been sold. There would be no notice in the paper, no farewell. The keys, he said, could stay in the till.

Eleanor sat on the bench by the bandstand and watched a flock of starlings shift across the sky. She did not yet know what she felt. What she knew was that the light, at least, would still come down through the maples next October, and the October after that, whether she was here to see it or not.
